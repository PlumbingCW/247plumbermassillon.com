Water is escaping and you cannot see where

Active leaks inside walls, floors and ceilings show up late. By the time a stain appears, water may have run for days. If a ceiling is sagging or dripping, shut off the main water valve and call us. If the ceiling holds water, poking a small hole over a bucket can relieve the weight, but only if you stay clear of the area under it.

Slow leaks are quieter and cost more over time. A water bill that jumps for no clear reason, a musty smell in a closet or warped baseboards all point to hidden water.

A high water bill

A bill that rises with no change in habits is the classic sign of a leak on the supply side.

A spinning meter

With every fixture off, a moving meter dial or flow indicator means water is leaving somewhere.

Damp or stained surfaces

Brown rings, bubbling paint and soft drywall mark where water has traveled.

Low pressure or a hissing sound

A leak in a supply line can drop pressure and make noise inside a wall.

Meter checks and high water bills

If your bill surprised you, try this. Shut off every tap and appliance, then look at the water meter. If the dial or flow indicator keeps moving, water is going somewhere. If it stops, the extra use came from a fixture, such as a running toilet, or from irrigation.

Water in Massillon comes from Aqua Ohio's Massillon Division for most addresses, so the bill and the meter belong to the utility. The pipe from the meter to the house belongs to you, which is why a leak outside the house is your repair. We find it, and for a service line repair we give you a clear price.

Massillon notes: old pipe and a high water table near the river

Older Massillon homes can still have galvanized steel supply lines. They corrode from the inside, form pinholes and leak slowly. Basement slab leaks are uncommon here, because most homes sit on basements or crawlspaces, so a wet spot on a floor is usually from a drain, a sump issue or groundwater.

Near the Tuscarawas River and its creeks, groundwater can seep into basements after storms. That is not a plumbing leak, and telling the two apart saves money. A sump pump may be the answer if the water appears only after rain.

Pressure tests on a supply system follow the pipe maker's rated limits, and repairs follow the standards in the Ohio Plumbing Code, OAC Chapter 4101:3.

Pool, spa and irrigation line leaks

A pool or spa that needs topping up every few days may be losing water through its own plumbing lines or through evaporation. The first step is to separate the two, and a plumber can pressure test the supply and return lines to see if they hold.

Lawn irrigation lines carry the same risk. A wet patch that stays green in a dry week, or a meter that moves while the system is off, points to a break between the valve and the head.
