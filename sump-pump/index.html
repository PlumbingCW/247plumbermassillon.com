Water rising in the basement during a storm

If the pit is full and the pump is silent, check that it is plugged in and the breaker has not tripped. Stay away from standing water if outlets or the panel might be wet, and call an electrician or us for guidance. Never wade into a basement with water near live electricity.

A pump that runs constantly may be overwhelmed by a high water table, or its float may be stuck. Both are fixable, and a battery backup can keep the pit pumping when the power goes out in the same storm.

Pump will not start

A stuck float, a failed switch or a dead motor are the usual causes.

Runs nonstop

Water is coming in faster than the pump can move, or the check valve has failed.

Loud grinding

Debris or worn bearings are damaging the impeller.

Pit is dry and the floor is wet

Groundwater may be entering somewhere other than the pit.

Standard homeowner's policies often exclude groundwater flooding. A water backup endorsement may cover pump failure damage. Check with your agent, and we can describe the failure in writing.

Why sump pumps matter in the Tuscarawas Valley

Massillon sits in the Tuscarawas River valley, and the city's history includes serious spring floods. The levees, viaducts and straightened channel built between 1939 and 1951 were the city's answer to those floods. They protect against the river and not against groundwater, so basements still take on water during wet springs, especially near creeks like Sippo Creek.

In Perry Township, the 2025 comprehensive plan notes wetlands and 100-year floodplain in the northwest and stormwater runoff problems in the north-central and northeast areas. If your home sits near those areas, a reliable pump with a backup is a basic protection. Test the pump each spring by pouring a bucket of water into the pit and confirming it starts.

Pump connections, check valves and discharge piping are installed according to the maker's instructions and the standards in the Ohio Plumbing Code, OAC Chapter 4101:3.
