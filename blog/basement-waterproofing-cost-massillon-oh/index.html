The short answer

There is no single average that fits a Massillon house. A basement with a failing sump pump needs a different fix from one with a cracked wall, and a house with a sewer backup needs a third. What you pay follows the cause, the size of the work area, the method and what is involved in restoring the floor afterward.

We do not publish a number because a number without a diagnosis misleads. We explain the factors so you can read any quote, from us or from someone else.

What changes the price most

A fair quote lists each item. A lump sum with no detail is hard to compare.

The largest drivers:

The source of the waterGroundwater, surface runoff and sewer backup each need different work.
Length of drain or wallMore linear feet of drain tile or sealing costs more.
Floor and wall workCutting concrete, trenching and patching are labor-heavy steps.
Pump and backupA primary pump, a battery backup and a discharge line add equipment.
Access and finishesA finished basement takes more work to reach than a bare one.

Standard homeowner's policies often exclude groundwater flooding. A water backup endorsement may cover pump or sewer backup. Ask your insurer before you sign.

Questions to ask any waterproofing contractor

  • What is causing the water, and how did you find out?
  • Which parts of the work are plumbing and which are masonry or grading?
  • What happens to my floor, and who restores it?
  • What backup power does the system have?
  • Is there a written warranty, and what voids it?
  • Who maintains the pump, and how often?

Be wary of a contractor who recommends the largest system before looking at the cause. A damp wall after rain may need gutters and grading, not a full drain system. A floor drain backup needs a line fix, and no interior drain will cure it.

Fixes that cost little and help a lot

Before you sign a large contract, try the simple steps. Clean the gutters and extend downspouts several feet from the house. Slope soil away from the foundation. Seal visible gaps around windows and utility entries. Test the sump pump and replace a weak one.

Those steps cost far less than a full system and cure a surprising number of damp basements. If water still appears after them, you know the problem is deeper, and a contractor's quote is easier to judge.

Comparing two quotes line by line

When two quotes differ by a lot, the gap is rarely profit. It is usually scope. One quote may include a battery backup, a new pit and floor restoration. The other may include only a pump swap. Lay the two side by side and mark each item that appears in one and not the other.

Then ask each contractor to explain why the item is or is not included. A good answer sounds like a reason about your basement. A weak answer sounds like a sales pitch. If a contractor will not break the price into parts, treat that as a warning.

Keep the written quote, the invoice and the warranty together. They matter if you sell the house, because a buyer's inspector will ask what was done and who stands behind it.

Finally, ask about timing. Work done in a wet spring is harder and may need drying time, and a contractor who books you in a dry month may give you a better result. If the basement is taking water now, ask what temporary steps will limit damage until the work starts.

Massillon note

Waterproofing in a river valley city

Spring floods damaged downtown Massillon so often that the city spent from 1939 to 1951 building levees, three viaducts and a widened river channel. After the 1913 flood, a relief fund raised $45,000. The river risk is lower now, and the groundwater that presses on a basement in a wet season is the part waterproofing has to handle.

Perry Township's 2025 plan lists wetlands and 100-year floodplain in the northwest and stormwater runoff problems in the northeast and north-central areas. Homes there, and low lying parts of Jackson Township near the Tuscarawas River, are the likeliest to need a pump. Permit rules vary, so ask the Massillon Building Department about any project.

Plumbing work in a waterproofing job follows the standards in the Ohio Plumbing Code, OAC Chapter 4101:3.
