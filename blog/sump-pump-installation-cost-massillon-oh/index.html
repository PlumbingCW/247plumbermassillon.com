Cost of Installing a Sump Pump in a Basement: Massillon, OH Price Guide

What a sump pump installation costs depends on the pump, the pit, the discharge and whether you add a backup. This guide explains each factor so you can compare quotes and avoid paying for work you do not need.

The short answer

We do not publish a single price because no two basements are the same. A simple swap of a pump in an existing pit is a short visit. A new pit in a concrete floor, with a discharge line and a backup system, is a project. What you pay is the sum of a handful of factors, and knowing them lets you read a quote.

Here is how each one changes the number, which choices matter most and what to ask a contractor before you agree.

The six factors that set the price

A good quote lists each of these separately. If one line is missing, ask about it.

The main drivers are:

Pump type and powerA primary pump is rated by horsepower and flow. A stronger pump moves more water and costs more.
New pit or existing pitCutting the slab, digging and setting a pit liner is labor and materials. An existing pit saves that.
Discharge lineA short run to a daylight drain is simple. A long run or a connection to a storm line is not.
Backup systemA battery or water-powered backup adds equipment and an extra installation step.
Check valve and alarmA quality check valve and a high water alarm are small items that matter.
Permits and inspectionSome work needs a permit, which adds a step and a fee.

Homeowner's policies often exclude groundwater flooding. A water backup endorsement may cover pump failure. Ask your agent, and keep your install records.

What to ask before you accept a quote

  • Is the pit new or reused, and who restores the floor?
  • Where does the discharge go and how far from the foundation?
  • Is there a check valve, and what type?
  • Does the quote include a backup, and how long does it run?
  • Who handles the permit, if one is needed?
  • What does the warranty cover and for how long?

A low quote that leaves out the check valve, the backup or the discharge work often grows. Compare quotes line by line, and ask what happens if the existing pit is cracked or undersized.

Common mistakes that add cost later

Discharging too close to the house sends water right back to the pit, so the pump runs constantly and wears out. Skipping a backup leaves you with no protection during a storm outage, which is when you need it most. Using a pump that is too small makes it run nonstop in a wet spring.

A cheap pump with a plastic float can jam and fail the first season. A better pump costs more upfront and lasts longer. If the pit is shared with a washing machine drain, ask whether the pump can handle that load.

Maintenance that keeps the cost from repeating

A pump that is tested and cleaned lasts longer, and the money you spent on installing it keeps paying off. Each spring, pour a few gallons of water into the pit and confirm the pump starts, empties the pit and shuts off. Lift the float by hand to confirm it moves freely. Check the discharge outside to be sure it is not frozen, blocked or buried.

Test the backup too. A battery that has sat for two years may not hold a charge, and you will not learn that until the power fails. Replace the battery on the schedule the maker gives, and note the date on the unit.

  • Test the pump every spring and again after a long dry spell
  • Clean the pit of silt and debris once a year
  • Check the discharge line for ice and blockage
  • Test the battery backup and note its age
  • Listen for a pump that runs often or makes new noises
Massillon note

Sump pumps in the Tuscarawas Valley

Massillon sits in a river valley with a history of spring floods. The 1913 flood was the worst on record for the city, and flood control work from 1939 to 1951 added levees, viaducts and a wider river channel in response. None of that stops groundwater from rising in a basement, which is the job of a pump.

In Perry Township, the 2025 comprehensive plan notes wetlands and 100-year floodplain in the northwest and stormwater runoff problems in the northeast and north-central areas. If you live there, a pump with a backup is a basic protection. Permit rules vary, so ask the Massillon Building Department if your project needs one.
