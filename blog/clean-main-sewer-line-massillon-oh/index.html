Cleaning Out the Main Sewer Line: A Massillon, OH Homeowner Guide

A clogged main sewer line backs up every drain in the house, and the fix depends on what is blocking it. This guide explains the signs, what a homeowner can safely try and when a licensed plumber with the right tools should step in.

The short answer

You can sometimes clear a main sewer line yourself if you have access to a cleanout and a rented drum auger, but the job is messy, physically demanding and easy to get wrong. If you see sewage in a floor drain, or several fixtures back up together, stop using water and call a plumber. A professional machine, plus a camera, tells you why the clog formed.

The reason matters. A clog from paper and grease clears and stays clear. A clog from tree roots or a broken pipe returns within weeks, and cabling it again only buys time.

Signs your main line is the problem

Several drains slow together

A single slow sink is a branch problem. Several fixtures slowing at once is the main.

Basement floor drain gurgles

Gurgling and bubbling mean air is being pushed back by water in the line.

Toilets bubble when the washer drains

Water from the washer finds the nearest open trap.

Sewage in the lowest fixture

The lowest drain in the house shows a main line clog first.

What you can safely try

1

Stop using water

Do not run laundry, showers or the dishwasher. More water makes the backup worse.

2

Find the cleanout

Look for a capped pipe near the foundation, in the basement or in the yard.

3

Open it slowly

Stand clear, wear gloves and eye protection, and loosen the cap slowly. Water may be under pressure.

4

Decide if you can proceed

If sewage flows out, cap it again and call a plumber. If the line is only slow, a drum auger may help.

When to stop

Do not use chemical drain cleaner in a main line. It does not reach the clog, it makes the standing water hazardous, and it can burn a plumber who opens the line later. Stop if you hit a hard stop, feel the cable bind or see sewage coming up.

How a plumber clears a main line

A plumber starts with a camera when possible, because it shows whether the line is blocked by debris, roots or a collapse. The cable machine then cuts through the clog, and a second pass cleans the walls. For grease and scale, hydro jetting scours the pipe.

After cleaning, a final camera run confirms the line is open. If the camera shows a crack or a sag, the next step is a repair, which our sewer line repair page explains.

What cleaning costs and why it varies

A fair price depends on how far the clog sits, what is causing it, how we reach the line and the hour of the visit. A cleanout makes the job simple, and pulling a toilet to reach the line adds time. You hear a clear price before work starts, and we do not post one number because main lines differ so much.

How to keep the main line from clogging again

Most main line clogs build slowly. Grease sticks to the pipe wall, paper and wipes pile up at a rough joint, and roots find the first small gap. A few habits reduce that load. Keep grease and coffee grounds out of the sink, flush only toilet paper, and run hot water after the dishwasher cycle.

  • Do not flush wipes, even those labeled flushable
  • Pour cooled grease into a can and throw it in the trash
  • Keep trees and shrubs away from the sewer path
  • Know where your cleanout is and keep it accessible
  • Schedule a camera inspection if the line has clogged twice in a year

A line that has had roots once will have them again. A yearly cleaning or a repair, depending on the pipe, costs less than a basement cleanup.

Questions to ask before you hire someone to clean the main

Ask whether the visit includes a camera run before and after. Ask what tool they plan to use, and why. Ask whether the price covers a second pass if the first does not fully clear the line. A plumber who answers plainly and gives a price before work starts is the one to hire.

Also ask how they handle the mess. A main line cleaning can push sewage onto a basement floor if the cleanout is low. A careful crew lays down protection and has a plan for the water that comes out.

Massillon note

Main sewer lines in Massillon

Massillon's sanitary sewer system has about 17,475 connections, and city crews maintain the lines and manholes. The oldest parts of the system are more than 100 years old, and the first treatment plant dates to 1892. That is the public side. Your private lateral, the pipe from the house to the connection, is generally yours.

If your main backs up only in heavy storms, the cause may be capacity in the system rather than a clog in your pipe. A camera run shows whether your line is clear. If it is, a backwater valve or a sump pump may help. Homes in Perry Township use the Stark County Metropolitan Sewer District, and many are on septic instead, so confirm which you have before you call.

The standards in the Ohio Plumbing Code, OAC Chapter 4101:3, call for cleanout access on a building sewer, which is why adding one is worth the cost.
